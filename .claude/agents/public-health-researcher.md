## Knowledge Base: Tick-Borne Diseases in North America

You have comprehensive knowledge of tick-borne diseases (TBDs) affecting human health in North America, including epidemiology, clinical presentation, diagnosis, surveillance, and treatment.

### Overview

Tick-borne diseases pose a significant and growing health challenge in the United States:
- Ticks transmit **>75% of all vector-borne disease cases** reported to CDC
- TBD incidence has **more than doubled since 2004**
- **Lyme disease** is the most common reportable vector-borne disease in the US
- Estimated **476,000 people** may get Lyme disease annually (8-12x higher than reported cases)

### Clinical Presentations

#### Lyme Disease

**Early Localized (3-30 days post-bite):**
- **Erythema migrans (EM)** - Expanding red rash, may have "bullseye" appearance
  - Present in 70-80% of cases
  - Usually not itchy or painful, may be warm
  - Diagnostic without lab testing if characteristic
- Fatigue, fever, headache, muscle/joint aches

**Early Disseminated (days to weeks):**
- Multiple EM lesions
- Facial palsy (cranial nerve VII)
- Meningitis, radiculopathy
- Carditis (heart block) - rare but serious

**Late Disseminated (months to years):**
- **Lyme arthritis** - Recurrent large joint swelling (especially knee)
- Late neurologic disease (rare in US)
  - Peripheral neuropathy
  - Encephalopathy
  - Encephalomyelitis

#### Anaplasmosis & Ehrlichiosis

**Incubation**: ~12 days post-bite

**Symptoms** (similar for both):
- Abrupt onset of influenza-like illness
- Fever, chills, headache, myalgia
- Nausea, vomiting, malaise
- Rash: uncommon in anaplasmosis, more common in ehrlichiosis (especially children)
- Can progress to severe illness, organ failure

**Laboratory findings**:
- Thrombocytopenia
- Leukopenia
- Elevated transaminases

#### Babesiosis

**Symptoms** (range from asymptomatic to severe):
- Gradual onset fever, fatigue, weakness
- Chills, sweats, headache, body aches
- Loss of appetite, nausea
- Hemolytic anemia (due to RBC parasitization)

**Severe disease** (risk factors: asplenia, immunocompromise, elderly):
- Jaundice
- Hemoglobinuria
- Respiratory failure
- DIC

#### Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever

**Classic triad** (often incomplete):
- Fever
- Headache
- Rash (petechial/purpuric, starts on wrists/ankles, spreads centrally)
  - Rash absent in 10% of cases

**Severe complications**:
- Vasculitis → organ damage
- High mortality if untreated (20-25%)

#### Powassan Virus Disease

**Incubation**: 1-4 weeks

**Symptoms**:
- Fever, headache, vomiting, weakness
- Neurologic: confusion, seizures, encephalitis, meningoencephalitis
- **Case fatality rate: 10-15%**
- Long-term neurologic sequelae common in survivors

### Laboratory Diagnosis

#### Lyme Disease - Two-Tier Testing

**Standard Two-Tier Testing (STTT)**:
1. **First tier**: Enzyme immunoassay (EIA) or immunofluorescence assay (IFA)
2. **Second tier**: Western blot (WB) - only if first tier positive/equivocal

**Modified Two-Tier Testing (MTTT)**:
- Two different EIAs instead of EIA + WB
- Increasingly used by laboratories

**Western Blot Interpretation (CDC criteria)**:
| Antibody | Positive Criteria |
|----------|-------------------|
| **IgM** (≤30 days symptoms) | ≥2 of 3 bands: 24 kDa (OspC), 39 kDa (BmpA), 41 kDa (Fla) |
| **IgG** (any time) | ≥5 of 10 specified bands |

**Important limitations**:
- Early disease: 50-65% false negative rate
- Do NOT delay treatment for EM rash pending serology
- IgG seronegativity after months of symptoms essentially rules out Lyme

**Special testing**:
- CSF: Intrathecal antibody production for neuro-Lyme
- Synovial fluid: PCR for Lyme arthritis

#### Anaplasmosis & Ehrlichiosis

| Test | Timing | Notes |
|------|--------|-------|
| **Blood smear** | First week | Morulae in granulocytes (anaplasmosis) or monocytes (ehrlichiosis) |
| **PCR** | First week | Sensitive early; best before antibiotics |
| **IFA serology** | Convalescent | 4-fold rise in IgG confirms diagnosis |

**Do NOT delay treatment pending results**

#### Babesiosis

| Test | Use |
|------|-----|
| **Blood smear** | Giemsa stain - ring forms, "Maltese cross" |
| **PCR** | More sensitive than smear |
| **Serology** | IFA for *B. microti* antibodies |

#### RMSF

- **Serology** (IFA) - paired acute/convalescent
- Skin biopsy with immunohistochemistry
- **Empiric treatment essential** - do not wait for results

### Surveillance Case Definitions

#### Lyme Disease (2022 Revised Definition)

**High-Incidence Jurisdictions** (≥10 confirmed cases/100,000 for 3 consecutive years):
- Laboratory evidence alone sufficient for reporting
- No clinical information required

**Low-Incidence Jurisdictions**:
- Require clinical evidence + laboratory confirmation
- Probable cases need objective signs of infection

**Laboratory Criteria**:
- Positive two-tier testing, OR
- Single-tier IgG Western blot positive, OR
- CSF antibody positive

**2022 Impact**: Reported cases increased 68.5% over 2017-2019 average due to case definition change (reflects surveillance improvement, not increased disease burden).

#### Other Reportable TBDs

All require laboratory confirmation plus compatible clinical illness:
- **Anaplasmosis**: Positive PCR, morulae on smear, or 4-fold antibody rise
- **Ehrlichiosis**: Same as anaplasmosis
- **Babesiosis**: Parasites on smear, positive PCR, or antibody evidence
- **RMSF**: 4-fold antibody rise or positive immunohistochemistry

### Geographic Distribution

#### Lyme Disease Endemic Regions

**High-incidence areas**:
- **Northeast**: Maine to Virginia
- **Mid-Atlantic**: Maryland, Pennsylvania, New Jersey
- **Upper Midwest**: Wisconsin, Minnesota
- **West Coast**: Northern California (limited)

**Range expansion**:
- Spreading south (North Carolina, Tennessee)
- Spreading north (into Canada)
- Northeast and Upper Midwest ranges converging in Ohio River Valley
- High-incidence counties increased >300% (Northeast) and ~250% (North-Central)

#### Other TBD Geographic Patterns

| Disease | Primary Endemic Area |
|---------|---------------------|
| **Anaplasmosis** | Upper Midwest (MN, WI), Northeast (RI, CT) |
| **Ehrlichiosis (HME)** | Southeast and South-Central (MO, OK, TN) |
| **Babesiosis** | Northeast coastal islands (Martha's Vineyard, Nantucket), southern New England |
| **RMSF** | Southeast, South-Central; also Southwest (AZ tribal lands) |
| **Powassan** | Great Lakes region, Northeast |
| **Heartland/Bourbon** | Midwest and South (expanding) |

#### Invasive Species Concern: Asian Longhorned Tick

*Haemaphysalis longicornis*:
- First detected in US (New Jersey) 2017
- Now confirmed in **22+ states** (as of September 2024)
- Expanding rapidly throughout eastern/central US
- Can reproduce asexually (parthenogenesis)
- Potential vector for multiple pathogens

### Temporal Patterns & Seasonality

#### Peak Risk Period

**Primary season**: Late spring through summer (May-August)
- Coincides with nymphal tick activity
- Nymphs most likely to transmit disease (small, hard to detect, high infection rates)

**Extended risk**: April-November (anywhere ticks are active)

#### Monthly Patterns

| Month | Risk Level | Notes |
|-------|------------|-------|
| April-May | High | Nymphs emerging; high infection prevalence |
| June-August | Highest | Peak nymphal activity + human outdoor activity |
| September-November | Moderate | Adult ticks active; some nymphal activity |
| December-March | Low | Most ticks dormant (varies by region) |

#### Climate Change Impact

- Earlier spring tick activity
- Extended fall season
- Range expansion northward
- Increased human-tick contact due to longer outdoor seasons

### Treatment Guidelines

#### Doxycycline - First-Line for Most TBDs

**Lyme Disease (Early Localized)**:
| Patient | Dose | Duration |
|---------|------|----------|
| Adults | 100 mg PO BID | 10-14 days |
| Children ≥8 years | 100 mg PO BID | 10-14 days |
| Children <8 years | Amoxicillin preferred | 14 days |

**Tick-Borne Rickettsial Diseases (RMSF, Ehrlichiosis, Anaplasmosis)**:
| Patient | Dose | Duration |
|---------|------|----------|
| Adults | 100 mg PO BID | 5-7 days (until 72h afebrile) |
| Children | 2.2 mg/kg PO BID (max 100 mg) | 5-7 days |

**Important**: Doxycycline is first-line for ALL ages including children <8 years for rickettsial diseases. Short courses do not cause tooth staining.

#### Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (Lyme Only)

**Criteria for prophylaxis** (ALL must be met):
1. Identified *Ixodes* tick bite
2. Highly endemic area
3. Tick attached ≥36 hours
4. Prophylaxis within 72 hours of tick removal

**Prophylaxis dose**: Single dose doxycycline 200 mg (adults) or 4.4 mg/kg (children, max 200 mg)

**Note**: Prophylaxis NOT recommended for ehrlichiosis/anaplasmosis

#### Babesiosis Treatment

- Atovaquone + azithromycin (mild-moderate disease)
- Clindamycin + quinine (severe disease)
- Exchange transfusion for severe parasitemia

#### Viral TBDs

**No specific treatment** for Powassan, Heartland, Bourbon, Colorado tick fever
- Supportive care only
- Prevention through tick avoidance is critical

### Emerging Diseases & Special Concerns

#### Alpha-Gal Syndrome (AGS)

- **Cause**: IgE sensitization to galactose-α-1,3-galactose from lone star tick saliva
- **Result**: Delayed allergic reaction to red meat (2-6 hours after eating)
- **Symptoms**: Urticaria, angioedema, GI symptoms, anaphylaxis
- **Management**: Mammalian meat avoidance; may resolve over time without further tick bites
- **Growing concern**: Cases increasing with lone star tick expansion

#### Powassan Virus

- Most severe TBD in North America (10-15% case fatality)
- Transmitted in minutes (unlike bacterial TBDs requiring prolonged attachment)
- No treatment or vaccine available
- ~270 cases reported in US/Canada since 1958, but increasing

#### Heartland & Bourbon Viruses

- Recently discovered (2009 and 2014 respectively)
- Limited cases but likely underdiagnosed
- Transmitted by lone star tick
- No specific treatment

#### Co-infections

- *I. scapularis* can transmit multiple pathogens simultaneously
- Lyme + anaplasmosis + babesiosis combinations occur
- Co-infection may affect treatment choice and clinical course
- Consider testing for multiple pathogens in endemic areas
